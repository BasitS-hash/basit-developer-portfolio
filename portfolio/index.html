The Scan Said Clean

gitleaks · PR #2227

I had been cleaning documentation for a password manager API I created. The examples included place holders for bearer tokens that were phony but appeared real enough for every scan to be flagged by gitleaks. The solution was to create an allowlist rule for the scanner to know those strings located within that file were allowed.

I created the rule. The scan was clear. Usually that would be the end of the story.

However, something did not sit well with me. The rule I wrote was more specific than "ignore this file" because it only needed to ignore certain specific known-fake values in the case that another condition held. While I got the correct result, I never took the opportunity to confirm that the scanner was actually still working on those files.

So I planted a secret. I placed a real-looking credential in one of the allowlisted files, the kind that should have tripped every rule gitleaks has.

The result was clean.

This was actually a much worse issue than what I had tried to mitigate--- the scanner didn't ignore the fake tokens; it completely skipped the files in question, meaning that any real secret placed in the file wouldn't be registered anywhere.

I had to delve further into the codebase than I had before. Gitleaks reaches decisions about a file in two distinct steps. First, it tries to establish whether to read the file at all or not -- this takes place at the enumeration phase. Second, for the files that were actually read, Gitleaks applies its rules of what constitutes a finding or not. My allowlist rules were interpreted differently by these two layers. While the detector saw two meaningful conditions established by my rule, the enumerator looked only for the answer to the question of whether any elements of the path pattern match or not. After this step, the enumerator just drops the file without running anything else against it after that. It is important to state that the detector does not disagree with its operation because it has never seen the file.

The fix is not complicated once you understand how it works. The enumerator has to learn to respect the condition for matching already respected by the detector so that the file can be skipped only if the whole rule applies. I posted this issue as a pull request together with a regression test confirming the fact that the problem arose due to lack of testing that would have revealed the conflict between these two layers of the software.

The aspect of the problem that I find most puzzling about this incident is that it was virtually impossible to detect that there was one. In fact, a scan that found nothing and a scan that did not scan anything at all provide exactly the same output. Both of them remain silent. Both of them provide the green results of the check. There is no reporting line "40 files skipped, all of them unread.\"

Most software products are able to report their failure. Security scanners are the exception, because success looks exactly like silence. The only way to tell a working scanner from a broken one is to test it against a problem you planted yourself.

The Benchmark Said It Passed

Model evaluation · federal health IT

The most important thing that I achieved through that project was that I was able to throw out a number that seemed right.

I was trying to find out whether an open-weight model could substitute a frontier model when it came to a certain task: the translation of a legacy analytics query language into contemporary SQL. There were enough reasons to want it to work. It was cheaper and could function within our ecosystem instead of calling an API of another company, which is very important in the world of regulatory compliance.

The aggregated accuracy was satisfactory enough to make us think that it was a fair decision to adopt the newly created instrument. It was the end of an assessment on the spreadsheet, but I decided to analyze the failures instead.

The failures I reviewed were not as I expected, as the model performed well in case of simpler queries, but on the difficult queries, the ones with complex structures and unusual scoping, it did not fail visibly. It returned clean, well-formed SQL that was simply wrong.

The distinction cannot be spotted by the benchmark anyway. Consequently, a model that crashes on 5 percent of queries and a model that returns confident wrong answers on 5 percent of queries end up with the same evaluation result. And the two cannot be compared. The first one notifies humans about failures, while the other one hides the failures in the results that seem correct, which means that humans cannot identify mistakes in the output.

The solution was not somehow better model, but rather a shift in what is considered to be evidence of translation correctness. Since legacy system was being replaced, it already contained correct answers. Instead of relying on the accuracy score for evaluations, we started comparing the outputs by translating the results issued by the legacy system, thus allowing us to catch mistakes.

Even though the accuracy level was kept the same, the situation where we did not need to rely on the newly created system anymore emerged.

I have encountered similar issues in another system before: there was a scanning system that did not find infected files, as it skipped analysis of them. A different task, yet the same problem was revealed -- a robust outcome where nothing lies behind is worse than an error, as error can at least direct your attention to the question, which must be checked.

The Principle With No Measurement

U.S. public policy · AI evaluation standards

On December 11, 2025, the Office of Management and Budget issued memorandum M-26-04, implementing Executive Order 14319 for the procurement of large language models by federal agencies. The memo establishes two Unbiased AI Principles that procured models must adhere to. The first principle is that the models must remain ideologically neutral. The second Unbiased AI Principle is truth-seeking, which is defined as an obligation for models to respond truthfully to user prompts seeking factual information or analysis, and to acknowledge uncertainty where reliable information is incomplete or contradictory. Federal agencies have until March 11, 2026 to modify their procurement policies accordingly.

Most of the commentary surrounding M-26-04 has focused primarily on the first Unbiased AI Principle, as that is where the political attention lies. I wish to argue, however, that the second principle of truth-seeking is the more difficult engineering problem, and that the memo does not provide agencies with a means to verify compliance with it.

A footnote within the memo highlights the potential significance of the truth-seeking principle. It notes that truth-seeking will likely be more relevant than ideological neutrality for LLMs that operate as part of a tool to summarize cybersecurity incidents for a security operations center. That description is directly applicable to a category of tool I have built, and I believe the recognition is important. When a model summarizes an incident for an analyst, no one is concerned about the politics of the model. The focus is on whether the summary accurately reflects the facts surrounding the incident, and whether the analyst can discern when it does not.

The disclosure system outlined in the memo does not answer that question. Four items must be requested from the vendor as a minimum threshold: an acceptable use policy; model, system, or data cards; end user resources; and a mechanism for end user feedback. The memo describes those cards as materials whose information "often includes summaries of the training process, identified risks and mitigations, and model evaluation scores on LLM benchmarks." Benchmark scores appear there as a characteristic of the artifact rather than as a required disclosure. Where the memo names them as something an agency should request, it is under an enhanced transparency threshold that agencies may invoke at their discretion, scoped to measuring "a model's bias, helpfulness, honesty, or accuracy when provided with ambiguous versus straightforward questions." That framing assumes the failures of interest are those triggered by ambiguous inputs. My experience suggests otherwise. The more serious failures do not occur under those circumstances.

Last summer I evaluated whether an open-weight model could substitute for a frontier model on a translation task for a federal health IT project. On completion, the aggregate accuracy of the two models appeared comparable, which is ordinarily where such an evaluation ends. Reviewing the individual failures changed the result significantly. On the most complex inputs the open-weight model did not crash, refuse, or produce malformed responses. It produced clean, confidently delivered output that was incorrect, on questions that had exactly one reasonable answer.

Nothing in the disclosure package M-26-04 describes would have surfaced that failure. A model card reporting high accuracy would have been truthful. A bias analysis would have returned nothing relevant. A benchmark testing behavior on ambiguous versus straightforward prompts would have measured the wrong axis, because the failures occurred on the straightforward prompts.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology has already identified the underlying phenomenon. In its Generative AI Profile published in July 2024, NIST AI 600-1, confabulation is listed as one of twelve risk categories specific to generative systems. The framework recognizes that these models produce confidently incorrect output. What has not carried through into procurement is any artifact that measures it. Confabulation is a claim about the character of a system's errors, while every disclosure this memo requests describes their quantity.

Three additions would help close the gap, and none require authority beyond what M-26-04 already asserts, since the memo establishes that agencies may require evaluation documentation as a condition of procurement.

The first would require vendors to characterize how their systems fail, rather than only counting failures. If a vendor states an overall accuracy of ninety-five percent, the appropriate follow-up concerns the remaining five percent. Does the system decline to answer, produce visibly malformed output, or produce output that a non-expert would accept as correct? Vendors conduct these evaluations already. Reporting the composition of the errors alongside their quantity gives a contracting officer something to reason about.

The second would require an evaluation against the process the system is replacing. Federal AI deployments generally do not create an entirely new capability; they automate work an agency already performs. That means the agency already holds a record of correct outcomes, and that record is an evaluation set specific to the actual deployment rather than to a public leaderboard. On my project the legacy system was the strongest evidence available, and it existed before the work began.

The third would require vendors to define a category of unverifiable output. In my case the solution was not a better model. It was deciding in advance what the pipeline would refuse to ship on its own, and routing anything it could not verify against the legacy output to a person. A solicitation can ask a vendor the same question: under what conditions does this system report uncertainty, and what becomes of that output. A vendor without an answer should not automatically be disqualified, but the agency should understand what it is accepting.

The reason the review step matters more in federal procurement than in commercial deployment is that federal AI systems almost always retain a human reviewer, and that reviewer is frequently the justification for approving the deployment at all. A human reviewer cannot identify errors that are not visibly detectable. Where a system's errors are indistinguishable from its correct outputs, the human in the loop is not an effective control. It is a procedural requirement that makes the approval easier to sign.

Ripple Effects of the Russia-Ukraine War: Unequal Impacts on Non-Belligerent Nations

Academic coursework · February 2026

The Russia-Ukraine War has produced cascading effects across almost every part of the world. Although the fighting itself is largely confined to Eastern Europe, the consequences for businesses and people globally are felt most sharply in countries that are not party to the conflict.

The effects on food systems fall most vividly on those most reliant on international trade. Approximately 30% of global wheat production is sourced from Russia and Ukraine, with Ukraine the primary supplier of corn to China, and Russia the largest supplier of grain to Turkey and Egypt. Agricultural exports from both countries have been significantly disrupted, hurting the poorest countries in Africa, the Middle East, and Latin America, which have little financial reserve or supply-chain diversification to absorb the shock. The FAO Food Price Index recorded its highest average increase on record in 2022, rising 14.3% over 2021, while 258 million people experienced acute food insecurity, also the highest figure recorded (Lin et al., 2023). Conflicts arising from imbalances of power among major European states have therefore produced disproportionate food insecurity in Africa and Latin America, driven by political decisions taken elsewhere.

Economic disruptions have compounded one another through rising inflation. Trevisan et al. (2022) note that the conflict began at an already depressed moment for global food systems, following the supply-chain disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic. The war added to existing vulnerabilities in agricultural markets already facing historically high demand and adverse weather from climate change, driving a severe increase in food and fuel costs that fell hardest on lower-income households, which spend a disproportionate share of income on essentials. The result was that the economic pain of the crisis concentrated on the most socially vulnerable populations within every affected country (Trevisan et al., 2022). Supply-chain disruption also reached manufacturing. Ukraine produces roughly 90% of the world's supply of high-purity neon, a required input for semiconductor fabrication (Rae, 2022), and the interruption of that supply delayed electronics and automobile production across Asia, Europe, and North America, raising costs for consumers globally. Roscoe et al. (2023) argue that these cascading failures expose an inherent fragility in global supply chains: the interdependence of nodes creates networks that become catastrophically brittle when any one node fails, and the resulting risk is distributed unevenly across national borders.

The war has also exposed weaknesses in labor markets that stable conditions had concealed. Ukrainian migrant workers form a substantial part of the Czech labor force (Rae, 2022), and their recall to military service raised the prospect of a labor shortage there. Developed economies have long benefited from the labor mobility globalization introduced, and from relatively stable labor supply lines; the war demonstrated how limited that resilience is. Welsh (2023) further notes that Russia's use of food exports for geopolitical ends cannot be overlooked. By supplying grain to states in Africa and the Middle East, Russia has exercised soft power and built political alliances intended to outlast the conflict. Globalization's advantages have not been distributed equally, and larger, more economically independent nations have proven far better able to absorb the economic effects of war than smaller states that depend on them (Lin et al., 2023).

Business as a Force for Positive Change: A U.S. Executive's Perspective

Considering these dynamics from the position of a U.S. corporate executive requires acknowledging the advantage American businesses hold in the world system. The United States played a major role in establishing the rules of international trade, in building global supply-chain infrastructure, and in developing corporate governance standards. That privileged position carries both opportunity and responsibility.

The first avenue is deliberate supply-chain diversification, as both risk management and social responsibility. Roscoe et al. (2023) support the conclusion that concentrating sourcing in a single region exposes a firm to precisely the kind of geopolitical disruption the war produced. Moving toward a multi-sourced model reduces that exposure. Where U.S. firms source from multiple suppliers across Africa, South Asia, and Latin America, they reduce their own risk while contributing to productivity and long-term economic development in those regions. The case is ethical as well as commercial, and increasingly it is becoming a practical necessity (Rasshyvalov et al., 2024).

Agricultural firms can help stabilize food supply chains and reduce food-insecurity risk. The conflict demonstrates that concentrated grain production creates a single point of catastrophic failure (Rae, 2022). Welsh (2023) recommends diversifying agricultural production across more countries to mitigate future crises of this kind. Large U.S. agribusinesses and food manufacturers should also invest in agricultural technology that distributes production capacity into food-insecure regions. This aligns with the re-globalization strategy advanced by WTO Director-General Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala, which favors developing broader and more diversified global markets (Rae, 2022). Firms investing in local food systems in vulnerable geographies act ethically and establish durable long-term markets for their own goods.

Firms should also examine their exposure to operations in states where human rights abuses and state control of enterprise create both moral and financial risk (Rae, 2022). Executives are responsible for due diligence sufficient to ensure their supply chains do not fund or otherwise support harmful activity. Zhao et al. (2024) argue that multinational corporations bear an ethical responsibility for the conduct of their global supply networks. Embedding social and environmental performance measures into supply-chain management is therefore both principled and prudent, reducing reputational and legal exposure while advancing human-rights protections in the regions where firms operate.

Finally, U.S. business leaders should use their political and financial influence to support multilateral trade policy that is both equitable and resilient. Although a populist movement toward protectionism has gained ground in Congress, excessive deglobalization would stunt global growth and drive inflation, with the heaviest costs falling on the poorest (Rae, 2022). Trevisan et al. (2022) caution that poorly managed trade liberalization can destroy the food and resource distribution systems that many developing countries depend on. Business has an active role to play in building a model of globalization that preserves the economic benefits of open trade while addressing weaponized interdependence, through strategic reserves, diversified sourcing requirements, and transparent supply-chain reporting (Rasshyvalov et al., 2024).

The war has demonstrated that while the benefits of globalization are distributed relatively evenly in peacetime, its costs are not when the political situation changes. American executives with significant global operations should therefore act now to help build a fairer and more resilient global economy than the one that preceded this conflict.
